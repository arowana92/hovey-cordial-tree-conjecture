# Which moduli always extend over two stars?

Fix any cordial labeling of a tree of order divisible by $k$. At any two prescribed old vertices, attach two new stars by one edge to each new center. The old vertices can coincide. When the star orders do not sum to $k$, an [existing construction](two-star-extension.md) always extends the labeling.

**Theorem.** For star orders $s,k-s$ with $0<s<k$, every such labeling extends if and only if the following holds. For each prime $p\mid k$ whose full power $p^{v_p(k)}$ divides $s$, some divisor $L\mid k$ satisfies
$$
   p\mid L,\qquad L\nmid s,\qquad
   \|s\|_L\ \ge\ L/p-1,\qquad
   \|s\|_L=\min_{j\in\mathbb Z}|s-jL|. \tag{1}
$$
Thus checking $1\le s\le k/2$ classifies the moduli that work for every pair of star orders. At even $k$, precisely the **powers of two and three times powers of two** work for all sizes. At odd $k$, (1) includes mixed-prime families such as $3^a5^b$ for every $a\ge2$, $b\ge1$. Modulus one is immediate.

When (1) fails, an explicit old star has a labeling that cannot be extended at the indicated sites. Its enlarged tree is a caterpillar and is itself cordial. The result concerns preserving prescribed labels in a tree induction. Hovey's unrestricted conjecture remains open.

## Reduction to an indicator on a cycle

Let $a,b$ be the old site labels and $D$ the unique deficient old edge weight. Translate all vertex labels by $-a$, so the site labels are $0,Q=b-a$ and the old deficit is $Q+P$, where $P=D-a-b$. Put $m=\operatorname{ord}(P)$. The [full-packet reduction](two-star-extension.md#one-full-period) constructs the labeling if $\gcd(s,k)\mid P$, if $Q\notin\langle P\rangle$, or if $m\nmid s$. For $Q=0$, put the smaller star first. Under failure of the gcd test the representative $1\le P<k$ exists. Take $h=1$ if $P>s$ and $h=-1$ otherwise. The positions $P-jh$, $1\le j<s$, avoid $0,h$ and give the required difference below. For $h=-1$ the only possible collision is $P=s=k/2$, covered by the gcd test.

In the remaining cases $m\mid s$, $Q\ne0$ belongs to $\langle P\rangle$, and $\gcd(s,k)\nmid P$. The forced-center lemma in the full-packet reduction shows that, after possibly exchanging the stars, the new centers must have labels $h,0$, with $h\ne0$. Write $B$ for the labels of the first star's leaves. The exact requirement in $G=\mathbb Z_k$ is
$$
 (\tau^h-1)1_B=e_0+e_{Q+P}-e_Q-e_{P-(s-1)h},
 \qquad |B|=s-1,\quad B\cap\{0,h\}=\varnothing. \tag{2}
$$
Here $e_z$ denotes a unit count at $z$ and $\tau^h$ translates by $h$. Exchange and renormalization replace $(s,Q)$ by $(k-s,-Q)$ and preserve $P$. The second star gets every remaining residue. We work in coordinates $0,1,\ldots,L-1$ on a subgroup $K$ generated by $h$ of order $L$. A consecutive occupied interval in these coordinates contributes minus its first point and plus the point just after its last. Any whole outside coset contributes zero.

### Why prime order is necessary

Fix a saturated prime $p$ as in (1), take $P$ of order $p$, and take any nonzero $Q\in\langle P\rangle$. Here $\gcd(s,k)\nmid P$ since $P$ has $p$-valuation exactly $v_p(k)-1$. In either orientation let $K=\langle h\rangle$ have size $L$. Summing (2) on each coset of $K$ gives the multiset equality $\{0,Q+P\}=\{Q,P\}$ in $G/K$. Hence $Q$ or $P$ belongs to $K$, and since each generates the same order-$p$ subgroup both belong. In particular $p\mid L$. Outside $K$ the indicator is constant on each coset. If $L\mid s$, the required local mass is $L-1$ modulo $L$, impossible with both centers excluded. Otherwise, with $r=s\bmod L$, the local mass is $r-1$.

Put $d=L/p$. At $Q$ in (2) there is a negative defect, since neither $0$ nor $Q+P$ equals $Q$. In a solution the coefficient there must be $-1$. Thus an occupied interval starts at $Q$, immediately after an empty interval. Each adjacent transition with positive sign is at $0$ or $Q+P$. In units of $h$ those two points and $Q$ have coordinates divisible by $d$, and both positive candidates differ from $Q$. The two intervals therefore have at least $d$ positions each. Consequently $r-1\ge d$ and $L-r+1\ge d$ in this orientation. This implies (1) in either orientation.

For a failed prime in (1), use an old star with $k$ vertices, labeled bijectively, with center $P=k/p$. Use the leaf labeled zero as first site and the center as second. The old sums omit exactly $2P$, hence the normalized data are $Q=P$ and discrepancy $P$. This realizes the obstruction on a finite tree. After deleting its leaves the enlarged tree has a path as its nonleaf core.

### A construction in the middle of a subgroup

Suppose $m\mid L\mid k$, $w=L/m\ge2$ and $\|s\|_L\ge w+1$. Take $h$ generating the order-$L$ subgroup with $wh=P$. This choice is possible since units modulo $L$ map onto units modulo $m$ (lift at each prime power and apply the Chinese remainder theorem). In units of $h$, write $Q=c h$, $w\le c\le L-w$. For $r=s\bmod L$, choose the orientation with $c\le L-r+1$. If this fails, then $L-c\le r+1$ in the other orientation. Use the following local positions for $B$:
$$
         [c,c+w-1]\ \cup\ [L-r+1+w,L-1].
$$
An interval with its lower endpoint above its upper endpoint is empty. The two intervals are disjoint, avoid $0,1$ and total $r-1$ points. Their translated differences give (2), including when an endpoint cancels. Add $\lfloor s/L\rfloor$ whole outside cosets to reach size $s-1$. There are enough since $s<k$.

Call a prime $\ell\mid k$ deficient in $s$ if $u=v_\ell(s)<v_\ell(k)$. If a deficient $\ell<m$ exists, take $L=\mathrm{lcm}(m,\ell^{u+1})$. Since $m\mid s$, this gives $L>m$, $L/\ell\mid s$ and $L\nmid s$. Both positive distances from $s$ to adjacent multiples of $L$ are at least $L/\ell>L/m$. The middle construction applies. If a deficient prime equals $m$, then $m$ is prime and $P$ has full prime-power divisibility from $k$ at every other prime and valuation $v_m(k)-1$ at $m$. This would give $\gcd(s,k)\mid P$. We may therefore assume that every prime dividing $m$ is saturated in $s$.

### Lifting the prime test to general discrepancies

Write $q=\operatorname{ord}(Q)>1$, choose a prime $p\mid q$, and assume (1). Since $q\mid m$, this $p$ is saturated. Choose a witness $L$ in (1) and set
$$
      M=\mathrm{lcm}(L,q),\qquad d=M/q=L/\gcd(L,q),\qquad f=\|s\|_M .
$$
We have $f\ge\|s\|_L\ge L/p-1\ge d-1$ and $f>0$. Also $q\mid s$ and $L\nmid s$, so $M>q$ and $d\ge2$. Both $s$ and $M$ contain the full $p$-power of $M$, while $d=M/q$ does not. This rules out $f=d$.

If $f=d-1$, orient the stars so $r=s\bmod M=M-d+1$. Choose $h$ generating the subgroup $K$ of order $M$ with $dh=Q$ in that orientation. Take positions $[d,M-1]$ in $K$ and add $\lfloor s/M\rfloor$ whole outside cosets. Now $(s-1)h=-Q$, so the last term of (2) cancels $e_{Q+P}$ and the interval gives exactly $e_0-e_Q$.

Suppose $f\ge d+1$. If $P\in K$, the middle construction works with $w=M/m\le d$. Indeed $w\ge2$ since $m\mid s$ and $M\nmid s$. If $P\notin K$, place the smaller star first, take $r=s\bmod M$ and choose $h$ with $dh=-Q$. In $K$ take the $d$ positions $-d,\ldots,-1$. They avoid $0,1$ and contribute $e_0-e_Q$. In $P+K$ take $r-1-d$ consecutive positions starting at $P-(s-1)h$. The next position after this interval is $P-dh=P+Q$. The difference of the interval supplies the remaining terms in (2), including a zero difference when the interval is empty. Take $\lfloor s/M\rfloor$ more whole cosets outside these two. There is room since $s\le k/2$, $r>0$ and $k/M\ge2$. This proves sufficiency and completes the theorem.

## All even moduli

Write $k=2^aN$ with $N$ odd and $a\ge1$. When $N=1$ the test is vacuous. For $N=3$, a split saturated at 2 has $s=2^a$ or $2^{a+1}$, and $L=6$ gives distance 2 as required. A split saturated at 3 has $v_2(s)=u<a$. For $L=3\cdot2^{u+1}$ its distance is $L/2\ge L/3-1$.

For other $N$, the [antipodal obstruction](two-star-semiprimes.md#antipodal-sites-at-every-even-modulus) suffices. It asks for $2^a\mid s$ and $\gcd(k/2,s^2-1)=1$. If $N$ is divisible by a square, use $s=2^a\prod_{p\mid N}p<k$. If $N>3$ is squarefree, choose $p\ge5$ dividing $N$. Take the nonzero residue $s\bmod k$ congruent to 2 modulo $p$ and to zero modulo $k/p$. In either case $s^2-1$ is coprime to $k/2$ and $0<s<k$.

## Two distinct prime factors

Write $k=p^a q^b$ with $p<q$ prime and $a,b\ge1$. All splits work exactly when for every $1\le z<q$ at least one $0\le i<a$ satisfies
$$
       p\,\|p^i z\|_q\ \ge\ q-\mathbf1_{\{b=1,\ i=a-1\}}. \tag{3}
$$

For a split saturated at $q$, the smaller prime $p$ is deficient. Taking $L=q p^{v_p(s)+1}$ in (1) gives $\|s\|_L\ge L/p>L/q$. For a split saturated at $p$, write $s=p^a q^v u$, $v<b$, $q\nmid u$. Try $L=p^j q^{v+1}$ with $1\le j\le a$. Then $\|s\|_L=p^j q^v\|p^{a-j}u\|_q$. The threshold in (1) is equivalent to $p\|p^{a-j}u\|_q\ge q$, with the right side lowered by one exactly when $j=1,v=0$. Thus (3) suffices with $z\equiv u\pmod q$. Conversely, take $s=p^a q^{b-1}z$. Every candidate $L$ for the smaller prime is one of the tested $p^j q^b$. This proves necessity.

For odd primes $p<q<2p$, (3) holds exactly when $a\ge2$, for any $b$. At $a=1$, $z=1$ fails. At $a\ge2$, only $z=\pm1$ can miss the strict threshold $p\|z\|_q\ge q$, and $\|p\|_q=q-p\ge2$. For $p=3,q=13$ there is no good exponent $a$ or $b$. The orbit of $1$ under multiplication by 3 has distances $1,3,4$ modulo 13. Choose the starting residue so that the last power in (3) lands at 1. Every strict test fails, and the last term also fails the weaker threshold.

It rebuilds labeled trees and recounts endpoint sums. It also checks the negative old-star examples through $k=200$ and compares the two closed classifications with (1) through $k=2400$.
