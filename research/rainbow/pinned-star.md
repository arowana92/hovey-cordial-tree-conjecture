# A divisibility obstruction for a prescribed forest

The restriction on the forests in the [rainbow test](README.md) matters for composite moduli. The following family characterizes one source of failure exactly.

**Proposition.** Fix $k\ge2$ and $1\le s\le k-1$. Take $k-1-s$ singleton leaves with boundary label zero and one rooted star on $s$ free vertices. Its center attaches to a prescribed boundary label $b$, and its other $s-1$ vertices are leaves. There is a bijection of the free vertices to the nonzero residues whose edge weights are distinct if and only if $b$ lies in $s(\mathbb Z/k\mathbb Z)$. Equivalently, $\gcd(s,k)$ divides an integer representative of $b$.

**Necessity.** Suppose $b\ne0$ and the center receives $x$. The weight $x$ cannot appear. A singleton would need vertex label $x$, a star leaf would need label zero, and the center edge would require $b=0$. Since the other $k-1$ weights are distinct, their sum is the sum $S$ of the entire group minus $x$. Summing the same weights as the labels of the free children plus the labels of their parents gives $S+(s-1)x+b$. Hence $b=-sx$. For $b=0$ the divisibility condition already holds.

**Sufficiency.** For $b\ne0$, choose $x$ with $sx=-b$ and assign it to the center. Write $j$ for the additive order of $x$, and write $s-1=qj+h$ with $0\le h<j$. Since $sx\ne0$, we have $h\le j-2$. For the $s-1$ star leaves, choose $-x,-2x,\ldots,-hx$ and $q$ whole cosets of the group generated by $x$ outside that subgroup. There are enough cosets since $s-1<k$. These labels avoid zero and $x$. Adding $x$ to them preserves the whole cosets and shifts the displayed segment one position. The center edge has weight $x+b=-hx$. Together, the star edges occupy exactly the leaf-label set together with zero, including when $h=0$. Give the singleton vertices the remaining nonzero labels other than $x$. Their weights equal their labels. The only missing weight is $x$.

If $b=0$, take $x=1$, label the star leaves $-1,-2,\ldots,-(s-1)$, and again give the singleton leaves the remaining nonzero labels other than 1. The star-leaf weights are $0,-1,\ldots,-(s-2)$ when $s\ge2$ and the center edge weighs 1. The resulting weights are distinct, also for $s=1$.

For $k=9$, the choices $s=3,6$ and $3\nmid b$ give twelve impossible pinned inputs. In every one the forest has seven vertices without children. The nine-label proof needs only forests with at most five. The obstruction concerns this stronger pinned extension requirement. The ordinary nine-label theorem allows the core and its coloring to be chosen.

## A sharp bound for shorter attachments at every composite modulus

**Theorem.** On a prescribed cordial core of order one modulo $k$, add a star by its center and any number of singleton leaves with one common old parent color. The center's old parent color is arbitrary. If the total number $t$ of new vertices is at most $k-2$, the labeling always extends and works on every parent-first prefix. At every composite $k$ the bound is sharp for a guarantee through order $t$. Indeed the full-size proposition above fails when the star has order a proper divisor of $k$ greater than one and its entering parent differs from the surplus by one. The [two-shift matching theorem](../biased/two-shift-holes.md) gives the same sharp short-length bound with two singleton-parent colors. At primes the [prime star theorem](prime-star.md) covers $k-1$ vertices even with independently chosen singleton-parent colors.

Translate the old surplus to zero. Write $a$ for the center's parent color, $b$ for the singleton-parent color, and $j$ for the number of star children. We need $t$ distinct nonzero labels and $t$ distinct edge weights. If $b\ne0$, put $b$ on the center. Give all other new vertices distinct labels outside $\{0,b,a\}$. There are at least $k-3\ge t-1$ available. Their edge weights are their labels plus $b$, and all avoid the entering weight $a+b$.

If $b=0$, put 1 on the center. Start with the child labels $C_0=\{-1,-2,\ldots,-j\}$ in $\mathbb Z_k$. If $a\notin C_0$, use $C=C_0$ and reserve $a+1$ from the singleton labels. The child weights are in $C\cup\{0\}$ and avoid $a+1$. If $a\in C_0$, use
$$
                 C=(\{-1,-2,\ldots,-j-1\}\setminus\{a\})
$$
and reserve $a$ from the singleton labels. Now $C+1\subseteq C\cup\{0,a\}$, and $a+1\in C\cup\{0\}$. No child edge weighs $a+1$, since $a\notin C$. In both cases $j\le k-3$ ensures that $C$ avoids 0 and 1. Choose the $t-1-j$ singleton labels outside $C$, zero, one and the reserved value. At least $k-j-3$ labels are available. Their weights equal their labels. This proves both balance conditions on every prefix.

## Several singleton-parent colors

Suppose one free vertex has children and the other free vertices are leaves. Prescribe a nonzero center label $x$ after normalizing the old surplus. Each new noncenter vertex has an effective shift $g$, its parent color plus any edge offset. Its weight is its own label plus $g$. If these shifts use at most $k-t-1$ different values, the labeling extends with the prescribed $x$. The center's entering edge may also have any prescribed shift.

To prove this, write a row for each of the $t$ vertices. Pad the list of shifts to length $k$, including one dummy of each noncenter shift. At least one further dummy remains whose shift can make the total zero. Hall's prescribed-differences theorem gives distinct sources and distinct destinations for all $k$ rows. Translate both permutations to put source $x$ at the center. If a real row now has source zero, it is a noncenter row. Exchange its source with that of the dummy having the same shift. This also exchanges their destinations. Removing all dummy rows leaves the requisite distinct nonzero labels and distinct weights. [Marshall Hall Jr., 1952](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9939-1952-0050579-7)

**Leaf-parent restriction.** Write the order of a smallest counterexample as $mk+1+t$ with $1\le t<k$. If it has exactly $t-1$ leaves and $d=k-t\ge2$, those leaves have **at least $d+1$ different parents**. For example, at order 51 and modulus 27, any counterexample with 22 leaves must have at least five leaf parents. The conclusion applies as well at every order in the same residue, for a smallest failure.

Indeed, suppose the leaves have at most $d$ parents. Remove all leaves and then a leaf of the remaining tree, which has order $mk+2\ge2$. This last removed vertex had at least one original leaf child, and its neighbor toward the remaining core survives. The exterior is one star and singleton leaves whose parents number at most $d-1$. Label the smaller core by minimality. For $d=2$ all singleton parents have a common color, so the short star theorem applies. For $d\ge3$, if any singleton parent has a nonzero color, give the center one such color. The child shifts then belong to the existing singleton shifts, and there are at most $d-1$ effective shifts in total outside the center. If all singleton parents have color zero or there are none, use center color one. There are then at most two shifts, again at most $d-1$. Apply the dummy-row construction.

The [whole-tree checker](check_short_stars.py) implements both constructions and rebuilds all edge weights from their endpoints. It checks all 56,420 pairs of prescribed colors and child-count cases at maximum short length for $3\le k\le22$, as well as 4,959 further trees using varied shifts and prescribed center labels through modulus 60.

python3 -B -m research.rainbow.check_short_stars

## Choosing which row receives the center

**Lemma.** On a prescribed cordial core of order one modulo $k$, normalize the surplus to zero. Fix the new center label $x\ne0$. The center's entering shift is $a$. All other new vertices are leaves, each with a prescribed effective shift, so its weight is its label plus that shift. For a child of the center this shift is $x$ plus its edge offset. Write $r_g$ for the number of required rows of shift $g$, including the center in $r_a$. Complete these counts to a list of $k$ shifts with sum zero using $q_g$ additional rows of shift $g$. Put
$$
 U=\{g:r_g>0,\ q_g=0\},\qquad M=\sum_{g\in U}r_g.
$$
All new vertices admit distinct nonzero labels and distinct weights with the prescribed center if either of the following holds.

* $r_a+q_a>M$.
* $U=\{a\}$ and the additive order of $x$ does not divide $r_a$.

**Proof.** Hall's prescribed-differences theorem gives a permutation of the residues with the specified list of differences. Regard its pairs as a source $v$ and a destination $v+g$. Let $A$ consist of all sources of shift $a$ and $B$ of all sources with a shift in $U$. Choose the center at a source $s\in A$ and translate every pair by $x-s$. The source originally at $s-x$ becomes zero. If $s-x\notin B$, its class contains a spare row. Discard this zero source as one of the spares of that class. The chosen center has nonzero source $x$, so it can be retained even if it belongs to the same class. Keep the remaining required multiplicities, assigning sources to the actual vertices of each shift. The destinations are distinct and are their actual edge weights. This also proves balance on every parent-first prefix. [Hall, 1952](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9939-1952-0050579-7)

Under the first hypothesis, $|A|>|B|$ ensures $A-x$ contains an element outside $B$. Under the second, $A=B$ and $|A|=r_a$. A failure to find such an element implies $A-x=A$. Then $A$ is a union of cosets of the subgroup generated by $x$, contradicting the divisibility hypothesis.

For example, with $t$ required rows and $d=k-t\ge1$, suppose the entering shift occurs among at most $d$ distinct noncenter shifts. Reserve one dummy for each of these shifts other than $a$, and use a final dummy to make the sum zero. Any extra dummies can be chosen before the final one. If $a$ receives a dummy then $U$ is empty. Otherwise $U=\{a\}$. Thus any generator $x$ satisfying the shift-count hypothesis works, since $0<r_a\le t<k$.

### Leaf counts at individual old parents

Suppose $d=k-t\ge3$. Let $v$ be the center's old neighbor, with $r$ of the new singleton leaves attached directly to $v$. Choose a set of at most $d-1$ old vertices containing $v$. If at most **$r+1$ singleton leaves** attach outside this set, the extension always exists, without restrictions on their parents.

Indeed, reserve a dummy for each distinct color of the chosen vertices. Choose a nonzero one of these colors as $x$ if possible. If they all have color zero, choose $x=1$ and reserve color one as well. This uses at most $d-1$ dummies because $d\ge3$. Complete the list with a final sum-correcting dummy. Every child of the center and every singleton at a chosen vertex uses a protected shift. Thus $M\le r+1$, while $r_a+q_a\ge r+2$ from the center, the $r$ leaves at $v$, and the dummy at its color. Apply the first part of the lemma.

In a least counterexample of order $mk+1+t$ with exactly $t-1$ leaves, remove all its leaves, choose any leaf $u$ of the remaining tree and let $v$ be its neighbor. Deleting $u$ gives a connected smaller core. Restore $u$ as the center. For every set of at most $d-1$ core vertices containing $v$, there must be at least $r+2$ original leaves whose parents lie outside both the set and $u$, where $r$ is the original leaf count at $v$. In particular, at order 51 and modulus 27, suppose there are 22 leaves and exactly five leaf parents, including $v$. The three parents other than $u$ and $v$ must **each** support at least $r+2$ leaves. Choose $v$ and the other two to test each one.

### An independently chosen leaf parent

The same permutation permits a second placement of the center. Use the formal shift $a+x$ for its row, translate its source to zero, discard source $x$, and then give the center its actual vertex label $x$. Its actual edge still has weight $a+x$. The two candidate source sets in the proof now differ by $+x$. Both the cardinality and the coset arguments apply unchanged.

**Theorem.** Let $d=k-t\ge3$. Select $h$ old vertices, $1\le h\le d-2$. Suppose one of them supports $r$ new singleton leaves. If at most $r+d-h-1$ singleton leaves attach outside these $h$ vertices, the star and all singleton leaves extend the prescribed core. The center may enter at any old vertex.

Let $b$ be the color at the selected parent supporting $r$ leaves, and $a$ the center's entering color. When $b\ne a$, put $x=b-a$ on the center and use the second placement. Its formal shift is exactly $b$. When $b=a$, use the original placement with any nonzero $x$. In both cases reserve one dummy for each of the selected colors and for $x$. This takes at most $h+1$ dummies. Of the $d$ available, keep one for the sum correction and allocate all other spare rows to $b$. There are at least $1+(d-1)-(h+1)=d-h-1$ dummies at $b$, counting the one already reserved. The class of shift $b$ contains at least $1+r+(d-h-1)=r+d-h$ possible center sources. Only the singleton leaves outside the chosen sites can have an unprotected shift. Their number is smaller than this class, proving the assertion.

**Restriction on ordinary counterexamples.** Let a least counterexample have order $mk+1+t$, exactly $t-1$ leaves and $d=k-t\ge3$. Denote the largest leaf-parent counts by $r_1\ge r_2\ge\cdots$. Pick $h\le d-2$ of these busiest parents, including the first. If a leaf $u$ of the tree obtained by stripping the original leaves lies outside the chosen parents, it supports $j\ge1$ original leaves. On deleting $u$ as well, apply the theorem to those $h$ old sites. The outside singleton leaves number $t-1-j-\sum_{i=1}^h r_i$. This must be at least $r_1+d-h$. Hence
$$
             j+r_1+\sum_{i=1}^h r_i\ \le\ t-d+h-1.
$$
The earlier distinct-parent bound supplies enough parents to select. The stripped tree has at least three leaves, since if it were a path the original tree would be a caterpillar. Every caterpillar is cordial at every modulus. Consequently one may always choose $u$ outside the busiest parent, and outside the busiest two when $d\ge4$. This gives $2r_1\le t-d-1$ and, for $d\ge4$, $2r_1+r_2\le t-d$. [Hovey's caterpillar theorem](https://doi.org/10.1016/0012-365X(91)90254-Y)

At modulus 27 with the maximum possible leaf count $t-1$, the two busiest parents together support at most **11** original leaves at residue 23 and **12** at residue 24, since $3(r_1+r_2)\le2(2r_1+r_2)$. At residue 25 the busiest parent supports at most **10**. In particular a 51-vertex counterexample has exactly 22 leaves and $2r_1+r_2\le19$. The checker built 19,683 complete trees of order 51 with four center children, seven and three leaves at the two chosen old colors, and eight further singleton leaves. It tested every triple of entering and chosen colors and used the second placement in 18,954 cases.

The [independent checks](check_star_parent_counts.py) enumerate every permutation and every proper nonempty choice of spare rows through modulus six. They verify 160,120 instances of the cardinality criterion and 82,862 further instances of the coset criterion by trying the center sources directly. Constructing and recounting whole trees through modulus 60 checked 7,980 ordinary attachments, including 788 beyond the earlier bound on the number of shifts, and 8,346 attachments using the coset criterion with prescribed shifts.

## A two-vertex path and 24 leaves at modulus 27

**Computer-assisted theorem.** On a prescribed cordial core of order one modulo 27, attach a path on two new vertices by one end to any old vertex. Also attach 24 independent leaves at old vertices using at most two prescribed parent colors. The coloring extends and is cordial on every parent-first prefix of the additions. Padding by singleton leaves and restricting proves the same for any smaller number of singletons. The result lifts to a multiple of 27 on a core of order one modulo that modulus when the singleton-parent labels take at most two values after reduction modulo 27.

Normalize the old surplus to zero. Let $a,b$ be the old colors for the singleton leaves and $p$ the old parent color for the path. Let $j$ of the 24 leaves use the second parent color. A row supplies the labels $x,y$ for the path in order from the root and a set of $j$ labels for the second group. All other nonzero labels go to the first group. The [verifier](check_chain_two_colors.py) reconstructs each of the 26 actual endpoint sums for all $27^3\cdot25=492{,}075$ literal inputs. Every row uses all 26 nonzero labels and has 26 different edge weights.

To generate a witness, try $x,y$ nonzero and distinct with $y\ne p$. Subtract $a$ from the weights of both leaf groups. On the cycles of adding $b-a$, the available leaf labels are every residue except $0,x,y$. In a consecutive segment of available labels ending just before a hole, the second group can use a suffix: the successor of any shifted label must also be shifted if it is available. An intact cycle can stay fixed or move in its entirety. The [generator](chain_two_colors.cpp) tries each suffix, discards collisions with the two path weights, and combines the resulting counts. At equal shifts it assigns any required subset to the second group. The existence proof only needs the verified rows.

The reproducible stream has five little-endian bytes per row. The lowest five bits encode $x$, the next five encode $y$, and the remaining bits give the second group's labels. The nesting order is $a,b,p,j$. Its SHA-256 is `c900058a5719c449270d518f3bb60bb491294d1569e4b3d5b2dc3b1cb5df0cba`.

~~~bash
set -euo pipefail
out=$(mktemp -d)
c++ -std=c++17 -O3 research/rainbow/chain_two_colors.cpp -o "$out/generate"
"$out/generate" "$out/chain"
python3 -B -m research.rainbow.check_chain_two_colors "$out/chain"
~~~
